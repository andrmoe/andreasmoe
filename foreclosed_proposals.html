Which AI Risk Proposals Have Expired?

Some proposals for reducing existential risk from AI could have been carried out when they were made but can't be now. They depended on something that has since happened and can't be undone: a capability level was passed, weights or research were published, or the field grew past the point where a few people could coordinate. This page collects prominent proposals, dates when each was implemented (if it was) and the event that closed it (if it has closed), and, for those still possible, names the event that would close them.

How to read the chart
  • The upper panel counts proposals over time: implemented and in force, still possible but not implemented, foreclosed and now much harder, or foreclosed and now impossible.
  • The lower panel has one row per proposal. The solid bar is the window in which it was possible, from when it was proposed to the event that closed it. After that, the row is hatched red if it can't be undone even in principle, or dashed amber if it is still possible but much costlier. Bars that run to today with an arrow are proposals that are still possible; hover to see what would close them. The bar turns green from the date the proposal was implemented, marked with a green square.
  • The middle panel shows rates as 1-year centred moving averages: how many proposals were made, implemented and foreclosed in the year around each date. The lines stop half a year from each end of the data, where the window would reach past it.
  • The horizontal axis can show calendar time or AI capability, measured as the training compute of the largest model trained so far. On the capability axis, years in which the frontier grew slowly are squeezed together, and the rates are counted per tenfold increase in compute instead of per year.
  • Hollow start markers mean the proposal date is approximate; hover to see which end dates are.

What the data says

  • The windows close faster. Most proposals from the 2010s stayed possible for years. Most of those made since 2023 that are old enough to judge closed within two years, because releases now follow each other so quickly that any line drawn just above the frontier is soon crossed, often by an open-weight model. (The medians above count only proposals that have closed; the two-year comparison also counts those still open.)
  • Releases are what close windows for good. All the proposals that are now impossible were foreclosed by a publication or a release: a paper, a set of weights, a model that passed a level. None was foreclosed by a government decision.
  • The "much harder" proposals depend on politics, not physics. Boxing frontier models, building tools instead of agents, coordinating the main developers, keeping AI out of AI development and denying China frontier compute would all come back within reach after a big enough shock, though at a much higher cost.
  • What gets implemented is mostly institutions and transparency. Safety institutes, an international science report, safety frameworks, reporting duties, whistleblower protections and a nuclear statement were adopted. The two implemented proposals that restricted capability itself, staged release and export controls, were both overtaken within a few years. No proposal that caps how capable AI can become has been adopted.
  • The proposals still open are mostly the ambitious ones. A halt to superintelligence, an international agency, compute oversight, chip verification and weight security are still achievable, but their closing events are the same forces that closed the others: algorithmic progress, chips spreading beyond tracking, open releases and AI doing AI research.
  • Anything indexed to a capability level has an expiry date. Compute thresholds, red lines and "if-then" commitments bind only until an open-weight model crosses the line, as already happened for self-replication and bioweapon-relevant capability. A useful question about any proposal is what event would close its window, and how soon that event is likely.

How the dataset is built

Inclusion threshold. A proposal is included if it meets all four tests: (1) Aim: it was put forward to reduce catastrophic or existential risk from AI, not only near-term harms. (2) Concreteness: it names something to do or not do, specific enough that one can tell whether it is still achievable. General calls for "cooperation" or "safety research" are left out. (3) Prominence: it came from a government or intergovernmental process, a frontier AI developer, a statement signed by several leading AI scientists, or a paper or report from an established research organization that is widely cited in the AI risk debate. (4) Feasibility: it was achievable when made. Each idea appears once, under its earliest prominent version. Proposals that pass are classed as implemented, still possible, now much harder (a datable event raised the cost far above what it was) or now impossible (a datable event made it unachievable). A proposal counts as implemented when it was adopted in binding form by at least one major jurisdiction, or carried out by most of the actors it was addressed to (for a company's proposal about itself, by that company). An implemented proposal can still be foreclosed later, as export controls were.

Judgement calls. (1) Dates. The start is the first public statement of the proposal I could attribute; the end is the first event after which it couldn't be carried out as proposed. Both are debatable, and several proposals were made in some form earlier. (2) Impossible vs much harder. "Impossible" means that undoing it would require un-publishing information or recalling distributed weights, or that the proposal referred to a capability level that has been passed. "Much harder" means that a policy could restore it, but would have to take something away from many existing users or actors. (3) The pause at GPT-4. The FLI letter asked to pause systems "more powerful than GPT-4". The window is taken to close with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the first widely available model clearly ahead of GPT-4 on most benchmarks. (4) Contested evidence. Two closing events are disputed: whether the Fudan self-replication result holds, and whether Kimi K2.5's benchmark scores mean real uplift. Both are included because developers of comparable closed models treated similar evidence as enough to act on. (5) Still possible. "Still possible" means achievable at roughly the cost it had when proposed, not likely to happen; some of these, such as a halt to superintelligence, have little political support. Their bars are censored at today, so their windows are left out of the medians. (6) Partial implementation. Implementation is often partial: the US reporting duty was revoked in January 2025 and replaced by EU and California rules later, and the Seoul commitments are voluntary and not all signatories met them. The date used is the first adoption that meets the definition; hover for the details. Lab-specific steps that most labs didn't follow, such as OpenAI hiding o1's reasoning, don't count. (7) Rates. The middle panel counts events in this dataset, not in the world. Counts are small (one event moves the average by 1 a year), recent proposals are easier to find and are probably over-represented, and proposals implemented and foreclosed on approximate dates are counted at those dates. Read the panel for its broad shape: almost nothing happened before 2023, then proposals, implementations and foreclosures all rose together. The rise matches independent measures (publications on AI safety roughly doubled from 2022 to 2023 in OpenAlex), but the shape after 2023 is uncertain: the last year is the least searched, and new proposals take time to become prominent. (8) Not exhaustive. The threshold is applied to proposals found in a broad search, followed by a targeted search for 2025 and 2026, but the AI risk literature is large, and more proposals pass it than are shown. Compute thresholds that only need lowering are not counted as foreclosed. (9) Capability axis. The capability axis uses the training compute of the largest model published so far, from Epoch AI's database of AI models (all confidence levels, models from 2011 on), interpolated log-linearly in time between successive records so that events between records don't collapse onto one point. Compute is used because it is the only capability measure that covers the whole period: METR's time horizons start with GPT-2 in 2019 and Epoch's Capabilities Index in 2023. It understates capability gains from better algorithms, so it moves slowly when efficiency rather than scale drives progress, as with DeepSeek's models. Several records rest on Epoch's speculative estimates, among them Wu Dao 2.0, Gemini 1.0 Ultra and Grok 4. No model has passed GPT-6 Astra's 1027 FLOP (September 2026), so everything after it sits at the right edge. (10) Restatements. Because each idea appears once, under its earliest prominent version, later restatements don't add rows. The targeted search found several in 2025 and 2026: MIRI's draft treaty, the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, the AI Regulatory Act, the AI Emergency Button Act, Amodei's call for mandatory testing and the UN's scientific panel. They are noted on the original rows. This is one reason the proposal rate falls after 2023 while the volume of AI risk writing keeps growing: the count is of new ideas, not of proposals.
