Is interpretability progressing fast enough?

Are we overcoming the obstacles to catastrophe prevention—and do comparable obstacles return as models become more capable?

Current judgment: useful progress, uncertain convergence. Internal tools are getting better at some fixed tasks. Evidence that these gains survive new concealment methods, stronger targets and a complete cost comparison remains limited. The record supports further investigation; it does not establish timely readiness or inevitable failure.

The original safety ambitions included systematic audits, feedback on internal processes, deliberate model design and scientific discovery. A decisive warning might require one consequential finding; broad assurance requires much more. There is no single technical threshold that every route must reach.

Measure progress and the moving target separately

The proposed metric: at a fixed total audit cost and false-alarm allowance, measure the fraction of concealed hazards caught by the best internally assisted audit and the best audit without internal access. Evaluate successive method vintages on both frozen targets and fresh, more capable targets. Report absolute detection rates and the fraction of baseline misses removed.

Fraction of baseline misses removed = (internal-assisted detection − baseline detection) / (1 − baseline detection)

This proposed measurement is not yet supplied by the results above. It is undefined when the baseline misses nothing, and can be negative. Always show the absolute rates and uncertainty; a large relative gain on a small denominator can mislead.

Measure target capability relevant to the threat, such as strategic concealment or autonomous task completion. Parameter count alone is unsuitable. Separate increases in capability from changes in hazard construction and attacker access. Repeated runs on the same targets do not supply independent evidence about new hazard families.

Publish benign controls, predictions, adjudications, target provenance, method versions, training and tool costs. Keep the claim allowance fixed. Then test whether findings enable correct interventions before harm. This would measure progress toward a useful safety decision rather than explanation quality alone.

What would change the safety priority?

  • Deprioritize the auditing route if several preregistered target cohorts show no material internal-access advantage at equal cost, or gains consistently disappear on held-out concealment processes—even under favorable assumptions.
  • Invest further if gains persist on fresh targets and stronger attacks, costs remain tolerable, and acting on the findings prevents failures in held-out end-to-end tasks.
  • Remain undecided when fixed-benchmark scores rise but transfer, false alarms or full costs are unmeasured. That is the present evidence state.

“Fast enough” needs a deadline and a route-specific safety threshold. Neither can be inferred from these scores. Evaluate plausible scenarios explicitly; the current evidence does not justify an arrival-date forecast. Failure of the auditing route would still leave training, construction, early warning and unforeseen discoveries to assess.
