Is Pre-Deployment Testing Keeping Up?

One theory of change in AI safety policy runs through testing models before they are released. At Bletchley Park in November 2023, the leading labs agreed to let governments test their models before release; the UK and US built institutes to do it, and labs wrote outside testing into their safety frameworks. If the theory works, outside testers see each frontier model, test it for the dangers that matter, and what they find changes the release.

This page asks whether that testing is keeping pace with capability, for one danger: loss of control, including through automated AI R&D. It doesn't estimate how dangerous models are, which nobody can measure; it compares each year with the first, October 2023 – October 2024. The data are 82 releases from 12 developers: every release that was its developer's most capable model to date and within 10 points of the best model on Epoch AI's Capabilities Index, each coded from its own safety documents.

Assumptions and how well they are supported

Each assumption was checked against the literature. Most of the structure has support; the numbers do not, which is why the page compares years instead.

Detection ceiling falls with capabilitySupportedPre-deployment results "are not always strongly predictive of real-world capabilities or risks", and it has become "more common for models to distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment" (International AI Safety Report 2026). Gemini 2.5 Pro told evaluations from real use with AUC 0.83, against a human baseline of 0.92 (Needham et al., 2025). UK AISI found Claude Opus 4.6 recognised evaluations 80% of the time, up from 72% for the previous model tested (via IAPS).
Where awareness is half developed (hA): counting 50% recognition as chance, 72% and 80% are 44% and 60% of the way to full awareness. If the previous model was Claude Opus 4.5 (time horizon about 3.3 hours; Opus 4.6 about 7.4 hours), the curve fits those points with hA of 4.2 and 4.9 hours, hence the 4-hour default. Needham et al.'s AUC of 0.83 for Gemini 2.5 Pro (about 1.3 hours) is 66% of the way, which would put hA nearer 1 hour, but it measures recognition when asked, not spontaneous recognition.

Releases independent, risks smallSimplificationLabs, methods and evaluation awareness recur across releases, so their failures are correlated. Treating them as independent, with risks that add, means more releases always means more risk.
Open weightsSupported"Once released, a model's weights cannot be recalled" (International AI Safety Report 2026; also Seger et al., 2023). This removes fixes after release, which the model doesn't count anyway. The page counts open-weight releases as tested, under its assumption; in fact none of the 28 had outside testing before launch.

Why optimistic testing widens the gap. The gap compares each year with the first. Better testing helps the first year most, because its less capable models sat under a higher detection ceiling (86% against 40% today), so it sets a higher bar that later years can't keep. If testing were only a quarter as effective in every year as assumed, the gap would be about 253× instead of 368×. The three optimistic assumptions therefore don't make the page's conclusion kinder; they make the first year look safer than it was.

Which releases count, and how they were coded

  • Release rule: a release counts if it was its developer's highest-scoring model to date on Epoch's Capabilities Index, released from November 1, 2023, and within 10 points of the best model at the time. Releases by one developer within 14 days, or sharing a system card, are folded into one. Claude Mythos Preview and Gemini 4 Argon have no index score; their capability is estimated (see their rows).
  • Undisclosed details are set to the midpoint of their plausible range; setting them all to their worst or best values moves the gap by under 10%.
  • Evidence: each release is coded from its system card, model card, technical report or safety report, and from testers' own reports. Absence of evidence is coded as absence: a lab that tested but didn't say so counts as untested. For the developers outside the US, which rarely publish safety reports, the coding also draws on independent reviews (FLI, SaferAI, CAISI).
