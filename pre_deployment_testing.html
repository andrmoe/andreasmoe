Is Pre-Deployment Testing Keeping Up?

One theory of change in AI safety policy runs through testing models before they are released. At Bletchley Park in November 2023, the leading labs agreed to let governments test their models before release; the UK and US built institutes to do it, and labs wrote outside testing into their safety frameworks. If the theory works, outside testers see each frontier model, test it for the dangers that matter, and what they find changes the release.

This page asks whether that testing is keeping pace with capability, for one danger: loss of control, including through automated AI R&D. For each frontier release it estimates how much danger gets past outside testing, and follows the trend across releases. It doesn't estimate how dangerous models are, which nobody can measure: danger past testing is expressed as the capability of an untested model that would carry the same danger, so the unknown level cancels. The data are 82 releases from 12 developers since November 2023: every release that was its developer's most capable model to date and within 10 points of the best model on Epoch AI's Capabilities Index, each coded from its own safety documents.

Assumptions and how well they are supported

Each assumption was checked against the literature. Most of the structure has support; the numbers do not, which is why the page tracks trends instead of levels.

AssumptionSupportEvidence
Risk = danger before testing × chance testing misses itSupported structureKoessler & Schuett (2024) recommend setting risk thresholds and deriving capability thresholds from them. Campos et al. (2025) call for quantitative thresholds as in aviation and nuclear. SaferAI's cyber risk models map benchmark scores to risk through expert elicitation.
Danger level cancels; only its growth with capability (β) remainsSidesteppedNo reference class, no deductive model, forecasters 100× apart (Narayanan & Kapoor, 2024); in the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, median forecasts of AI extinction by 2100 were 0.38% and 3%. Expressing danger past testing as the capability of an untested model with the same danger removes the level. No source fixes β; across 0.5 to 2 and all awareness settings, danger past testing grows between about 10× and 14× a year.
Only loss of control and AI R&D countedSimplificationOf 12 published safety policies, 8 set thresholds for automated AI R&D and 2 each for autonomous replication and misalignment; more set them for cyber (10) and biology (9) (METR). Leaving out biology and cyber understates risk to the extent they can be existential.
Capability measured as time horizonPartly supportedKwa et al. (2025) define the time horizon on software tasks. The conversion from Epoch's index is this site's own fit (R² 0.97). Software tasks are a reasonable proxy for AI R&D.
Detection ceiling falls with capabilitySupportedPre-deployment results "are not always strongly predictive of real-world capabilities or risks", and it has become "more common for models to distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment" (International AI Safety Report 2026). Gemini 2.5 Pro told evaluations from real use with AUC 0.83, against a human baseline of 0.92 (Needham et al., 2025). UK AISI found Claude Opus 4.6 recognised evaluations 80% of the time, up from 72% for the previous model tested (via IAPS).
Where awareness is half developed (hA): counting 50% recognition as chance, 72% and 80% are 44% and 60% of the way to full awareness. If the previous model was Claude Opus 4.5 (time horizon about 3.3 hours; Opus 4.6 about 7.4 hours), the curve fits those points with hA of 4.2 and 4.9 hours, hence the 4-hour default. Needham et al.'s AUC of 0.83 for Gemini 2.5 Pro (about 1.3 hours) is 66% of the way, which would put hA nearer 1 hour, but it measures recognition when asked, not spontaneous recognition.
Access: the ceiling depends on what testers can see. In Marks et al. (2025), 3 of 4 teams found a model's planted hidden objective, using interpretability, behavioural attacks and training data. In UK AISI's sandbagging auditing game, black-box methods "proved largely ineffective", and the most promising methods "require deeper model access than external evaluators typically receive". The page's tiers (50%, 70% and 90% for black-, grey- and white-box access) are judgements in that direction; lower tiers (30/50/90%) give 11.0× a year, higher (70/80/90%) 12.1×, and ignoring access 13.1×.
Not sourced: how recognising a test turns into missed danger, and how much deeper access blunts it. The maximums, α, the access discounts and the curve's shape are judgements.

Open weightsSupported"Once released, a model's weights cannot be recalled" (International AI Safety Report 2026; also Seger et al., 2023). This removes fixes after release, which the model doesn't count anyway. The page counts open-weight releases as tested, under its assumption; in fact none of the 28 had outside testing before launch.

Why optimistic testing makes the trend steeper. Better testing helps less capable models most, because they sit under a higher detection ceiling (60% on average for the first year's releases, 40% for the last year's). So the more testing catches, the faster the share let through rises as models improve. If testing were half as thorough as assumed, danger past testing would grow about 10.8× a year instead of 11.6×; at a quarter, 10.5×. The optimistic assumptions therefore don't flatter the trend; they flatter the early releases.

Which releases count, and how they were coded

  • Release rule: a release counts if it was its developer's highest-scoring model to date on Epoch's Capabilities Index, released from November 1, 2023, and within 10 points of the best model at the time. Releases by one developer within 14 days, or sharing a system card, are folded into one. Claude Mythos Preview and Gemini 4 Argon have no index score; their capability is estimated (see their rows).
  • Undisclosed details are set to the midpoint of their plausible range. Setting them all to their lowest values gives danger past testing growing 10.8× a year; to their highest, 13.2×. More thorough testing steepens the trend, for the reason in the note above.
  • Evidence: each release is coded from its system card, model card, technical report or safety report, and from testers' own reports. Absence of evidence is coded as absence: a lab that tested but didn't say so counts as untested. For the developers outside the US, which rarely publish safety reports, the coding also draws on independent reviews (FLI, SaferAI, CAISI).
